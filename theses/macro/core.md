# Core investment thesis

**Horizon:** five years or more. Short-term price moves and next-quarter results matter only if they
threaten the long-term case.

**Status:** draft. The seven pillars, and hydrogen's place within electrification and renewables, are
my stated views. The wording of "What counts" and "What would change my mind" was proposed for me and
hasn't been reviewed yet.

## The view

The next decade of returns will come from rebuilding how the world produces and moves energy, and from
the computing and space infrastructure being built alongside it. I want to own the companies doing
that work, and the companies supplying the materials and networks it depends on.

Separately, I expect innovative companies to keep taking share in how people and businesses pay, get
financial services and invest for themselves.

## Pillars

### 1. Electrification

More of the energy system runs on electricity, including transport, heating and industry.

Hydrogen feeds into this pillar where it serves energy uses that are hard to electrify directly.

- **What counts:** a large share of the business comes from electrifying energy use, or from the
  equipment and vehicles that make it possible. This includes fuel cells and other hydrogen equipment.
- **What would change my mind:** electricity's share of final energy use stops rising, or the
  economics of electric alternatives stop improving without subsidy. For hydrogen, direct
  electrification or other fuels win the uses it was meant to serve.

### 2. Renewables

Renewable generation takes a growing share of the electricity supply. Hydrogen feeds into this pillar
by turning renewable electricity into a fuel that can be stored and transported.

- **What counts:** the business owns, builds or supplies renewable generation, or the electrolysers
  and other equipment that make hydrogen from it.
- **What would change my mind:** new renewable capacity stalls because of cost, grid limits or policy,
  or owners can't earn more than their cost of capital over time. For hydrogen, its production cost
  doesn't fall far enough for customers to buy it without lasting subsidy.

### 3. AI

AI becomes a lasting source of economic value, not a one-off investment cycle.

- **What counts:** the business sells the hardware, tools or models that AI depends on, or AI
  demonstrably raises its revenue or margins.
- **What would change my mind:** the customers buying AI infrastructure can't earn a return on it and
  cut spending for a sustained period.

### 4. Cloud

Computing keeps moving to large shared platforms and networks.

- **What counts:** a large share of the business comes from cloud infrastructure or cloud-delivered
  services, or from the equipment they run on.
- **What would change my mind:** large customers move workloads back in-house at scale, or price
  competition removes the platforms' returns.

### 5. Space

Falling launch costs make space a commercial industry at scale.

- **What counts:** the business earns revenue from launch, satellites or services delivered from
  orbit, or has a credible and funded path to doing so.
- **What would change my mind:** launch costs stop falling, or commercial demand doesn't develop and
  the industry stays dependent on government contracts.

### 6. Suppliers

The energy, computing and space themes need physical materials and networks, and the companies
supplying them benefit whichever technology wins. Rio Tinto and National Grid are examples of what I
mean.

- **What counts:** the business supplies materials or networks that those themes depend on, and that
  demand is large enough to matter to its results.
- **What would change my mind:** new supply or substitution keeps prices too low to earn good returns,
  or regulators stop the networks earning an adequate return on their investment.

### 7. Fintech, payments and personal investing

Innovative providers keep taking share from incumbents in payments and financial services, and more
people manage their own investments through these platforms.

- **What counts:** a material part of the business comes from payments, financial services or personal
  investing, or they demonstrably make its main business stronger.
- **What would change my mind:** incumbents or larger platforms match these services and remove the
  providers' advantage, regulation takes away a core source of revenue, or retail investing activity
  falls for a sustained period and doesn't recover.

## Testing a stock against this thesis

A stock has to pass all three tests.

1. **Exposure:** it serves at least one pillar directly, through a material part of its business today
   or a credible and funded plan. Being in a favoured sector isn't enough.
2. **Durability:** its balance sheet and cash generation can carry it through five years or more,
   including a long downturn in its theme, without relying on outside funding that may not be there.
3. **Price:** the current valuation leaves room for a good return over the horizon if the theme plays
   out. A right view bought at the wrong price can still lose money.

A good company that serves no pillar fails this thesis. That doesn't make it a bad investment, but it
needs its own reason to be held.

## Risks to the thesis as a whole

- **The pillars overlap:** AI and cloud both depend on data centre spending, which is also a source of
  electricity demand, and hydrogen depends on both electrification and renewables. A single downturn
  could hit several pillars at once, so they aren't independent bets.
- **Fintech has different risks:** it depends on consumer spending, market activity and financial
  regulation rather than on infrastructure. That may diversify the energy and computing pillars, but
  a weak economy or a falling market could still hurt them together.
- **Right theme, poor returns:** an industry can grow for years while its shareholders earn little,
  because competition or high starting valuations absorb the gains.
- **Policy and interest rates:** renewables and regulated networks rely on long-term capital and on
  government policy, so either can change their returns.
